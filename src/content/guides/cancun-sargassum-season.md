Every summer I get the same message from friends planning a trip: "Is the seaweed bad right now?" Here's how the season works, what it looks like as of November 2026, and how to pick a beach and a hotel so it doesn't ruin your vacation.

## What sargassum is and where it comes from

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that floats in huge mats across the Atlantic, in a belt that stretches from West Africa to the Caribbean. Wind and currents push it west, and some of it ends up on the beaches of the Mexican Caribbean, from Cancún down to Mahahual.

It isn't pollution from the hotels and it isn't something Cancún can switch off. The state, the Mexican Navy (SEMAR) and the hotels put up floating barriers at sea and clean the beaches, but in a bad week more arrives than they can clear.

## Sargassum season, month by month

The director of the Quintana Roo sargassum monitoring network, Esteban Amaro, puts the worst months at **May, June and July**, with amounts falling in August and arrivals continuing into September and part of October. UNAM's sargassum research group calls **November to March** the low season, with **December to February** the least likely months for big piles.

| Months | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Nov–Mar | Low season. Usually clear beaches. |
| Apr–May | It can start building. |
| Jun–Jul | Peak. Some beaches get thick piles. |
| Aug–Oct | Fading, but uneven from beach to beach. |

## 2026 was a record year

This year didn't follow the calendar. According to the state environment department (SEMA), seaweed started landing in **January** instead of March, and by September 30 crews had collected **138,000 tonnes** in Quintana Roo, compared with 92,782 tonnes in all of 2025.

The good news is that it did ease off. On October 8, 2026, SEMAR lowered its alert to green (low), and the University of South Florida's August outlook expected amounts in the region to keep falling "until possibly November", with beaching continuing but less severe.

## What the map says as of November 2026

The official SEMA semáforo checks 100 beaches in the north of the state and 40 in the south. On the **November 11, 2026** report:

- **North:** 86 beaches low, 3 with none, 8 moderate, 3 abundant and 0 excessive.
- **Cancún:** every Hotel Zone beach on the list was low except **Playa Coral**, near Punta Nizuc, which was abundant.
- **Isla Mujeres:** Playa Norte, Playa Centro, Playa Mia, Lancheros and Punta Sur were all low.
- **Still messy:** Puerto Morelos Norte and parts of Cozumel's east side were moderate or abundant, and the far south (Xcalak, Bacalar Chico) was excessive.

So if you're coming this winter, seaweed is unlikely to be your problem. My [seaweed map](/tools/seaweed-map) shows every beach on that report, pin by pin, and I update it when a new semáforo comes out.

## Which beaches and areas get less seaweed

Look at which way the beach faces. The beaches at the start of the Hotel Zone, **Las Perlas, Langosta, Tortugas and Caracol**, face north into the Bahía de Mujeres, and they are known for getting much less sargassum than the long open-sea stretch from Km 9 to Km 22. The same goes for **Playa Norte** on Isla Mujeres and the calm water at **Costa Mujeres**.

That's also why every hotel page on this site shows a beach type worked out from the hotel's location: hotels on the north-facing bay side are marked "Calm bay" with usually much less seaweed, and most pages also show the nearest beach on the latest official report.

## How to handle a seaweed day

- **Go early.** Crews rake most beaches in the morning, so the first hours are usually the cleanest.
- **Switch beaches.** Levels can be very different a few kilometers apart. Check the map and take the bus or a taxi to a low beach.
- **Take the ferry.** A day on [Isla Mujeres](/tours/isla-mujeres-day-trip) is easy and cheap, and Playa Norte faces the calm side.
- **Swim inland.** A [cenote tour](/tours/cenote-tour) is seaweed-free all year.
- **Book a good pool.** From May to August, a hotel with great pools matters more than the beach photos.

Don't sit next to big rotting piles. The US EPA says decaying sargassum releases hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, and it can irritate your airways, especially for kids, older people and anyone with asthma.

## My advice

If you can choose, come between November and March. If summer is when you can travel, still come, but stay on the bay side or keep Isla Mujeres and the cenotes as your plan B. And check the [seaweed map](/tools/seaweed-map) a few days before you fly: a "low" beach isn't a guarantee, but it's the best picture we have.
